SPIRo: Soft Pipe Inspection Robot

A pneumatically actuated soft robot that crawls along and climbs gas pipelines to find leaks in hard-to-reach places. It combines a compliant scissor linkage, McKibben artificial muscles, and origami-inspired magnetic grippers with a multimodal machine-learning model that fuses thermal images and gas-sensor data.

Problem

Industrial gas leak detection methods such as hydrostatic testing and camera inspection are slow, expensive, and labor-intensive. In the U.S. alone, a significant pipeline leak occurs every 40 hours, and smaller fugitive leaks often go undetected, contributing to nearly seven million tons of methane released each year. Methane is about 30 times more potent than carbon dioxide in global warming potential, so catching leaks early is one of the most efficient ways to cut emissions.

Robot design

SPIRo uses a compliant scissor linkage, McKibben artificial muscle actuators, and magnetic grippers. It crawls at 15 mm/s on a horizontal surface and climbs at 9 mm/s on a vertical one, and it can bend its body 60 degrees vertically and 40 degrees horizontally to clear obstacles. The origami-inspired grippers support 417 grams of extra payload for leak detection sensors and equipment.

Leak detection model

The robot runs a multimodal deep feature fusion model with a deep forest ensemble. It combines the convolutional layers of three CNNs (AlexNet, ResNet-50, and MobileNet) and analyzes both thermal images and gas sensor data, which makes it more accurate and more reliable through redundancy. The model is also memory efficient, so it suits real-time detection.

Field testing

To get SPIRo ready for field use, I consulted labs and industry professionals across the country and earned a chance to test at the Methane Emissions Technology Evaluation Center (METEC), which waived its usual testing costs. SPIRo detected leaks with 95% accuracy across those trials. I also worked with the MIT Media Lab to integrate FlowIO, a standalone air compressor system, and with the Southwest Research Institute to calibrate the robot for tests at home.

Papers and patents from this project are listed under Publications & Patents. I co-founded Enviro-AI to bring SPIRo to market.

Problem

Heart failure is a chronic condition in which the heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. Severe (class III or IV) cases often call for a heart transplant, but donor hearts are far scarcer than the demand, and many patients aren't eligible because of age or other conditions. That leaves a real need for better ventricular assist devices (VADs).

Approach

Unlike rigid robots, soft robots are made of flexible, deformable materials that can work safely in delicate environments. This cardiac assist system pairs HASEL actuators, which can self-heal for better reliability, with AI-based control. Multi-material 3D printing gives precise, reproducible geometries, and a machine-learning controller handles the actuators' nonlinear behavior, predicting stress with an R² of 0.91. The device reaches a maximum strain of 32.9% and a maximum energy output of 5.49 N·mm, which exceeds the requirements for cardiac assist. Using DIW 3D printing and soft materials also makes the devices easier to personalize.

Papers from this project are listed under Publications & Patents. I'm now founding Pulsar Robotics to develop it further.

Amphibious Shape-Morphing Robotic Turtle

A turtle-inspired robot whose limbs switch between hydrodynamic flippers for swimming and load-bearing legs for walking, using pressure-responsive laminar jamming to change shape and stiffness. Built for deep water, with a pressure-rated chassis and active buoyancy control.

Mobile robots matter for search and rescue, environmental monitoring, and home service, but most are built for a single environment, with dedicated mechanisms such as paddles or wheels. Animals instead adapt their anatomy, stiffness, and behavior to move through many environments.

This robot's limbs are inspired by sea turtles and land tortoises. Pressure-responsive laminar jamming lets them change quickly and efficiently between hydrodynamic flippers optimized for swimming and load-bearing legs for walking on land.

For deep-water swimming, the motors and chassis are designed to withstand 30 PSI, equivalent to a depth of 10 meters. Integrated buoyancy control pumps let the turtle hold neutral buoyancy or vary it to dive and ascend.

Final Preparations

Loading the rocket.

↔ Scroll, drag, or use the arrows to see more. Click any photo to enlarge it.

Over five months I led our first build: researching, proposing designs, and getting feedback at each step. The first launch flew straight, but altimeter data showed we'd fallen slightly short of our target altitude, so we removed weight. That shifted the center of gravity and destabilized the rocket, which careened across the field and broke apart on the next launch.

We traced the failure to the center-of-gravity shift, rebuilt the rocket from spare components in the five days before our next launch window, and flew two successful launches the following weekend.

The RC airplane is custom built from foam board and uses gyro stabilization for smooth flight.

Teaching & Outreach

Robotics Curriculum for Elementary Schools

Built a hands-on robotics curriculum with a teacher at Henry Ford Elementary, a Title 1 school, and founded a district-wide FIRST LEGO League competition for eight teams. Over 60 students took part, and we raised $4.5K for kits and an after-school robotics program. Run through my nonprofit, Cream of the Crop.

Workshop video.

↔ Scroll, drag, or use the arrows to see more. Click any photo to enlarge it.

When I was conducting robotics demos, I was approached by a teacher at Henry Ford Elementary who was interested in furthering the depth of robotics in her STEM program. She asked how to get students excited and shared her struggles. Listening, I realized that her problem was systemic: the curriculum was fixated on worksheets rather than wonder.

We began proposing changes. I introduced a hands-on Robotics curriculum and founded a district-wide FIRST LEGO League competition for eight teams. Rather than prescribing instructions, we encouraged testing unique ideas, and excitement overtook boredom as over 60 students huddled over sensors, cheered the robot on, and laughed at code going wrong.

We've now continued our program into the school year, having raised $4.5K to provide kits and materials for our classes and creating an after school enrichment program and LEGO robotics league for Henry Ford's students.

RC Airplane Workshop

Taught eight middle school students to build RC airplanes from foam board, then flight-tested them and tuned wing geometry and center of gravity until the planes flew.

↔ Scroll, drag, or use the arrows to see more. Click any photo to enlarge it.

I led a remote-control airplane workshop at a local library, with 8 middle school students participating. I taught the class to build RC airplanes from scratch using foamboard. We then took a fieldtrip to a local flying park to test them together. But after hours of cutting and gluing, many of the class's planes came crashing down after wobbling just a few feet. I gathered the students around to try and identify problems, patching the battered airframes and wings while making adjustments based off of each test flight. Soon, my students began offering suggestions, changing the geometry of the wings on their own or shifting the center of gravity around, trying to identify the ideal setup. Our on-the-spot fixes weren't exactly pretty, but our planes flew beautifully through the air.
